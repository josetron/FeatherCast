Field Method

How To Determine A Bird Fallout

A fallout is most likely when heavy migration meets a weather stop sign, then fresh ground reports confirm birds are landing instead of passing over.

1

Monitor Radar For The Wall

Watch BirdCast or radar for dense northbound movement that stalls near rain, storms, or a front. High birds-in-flight with slower crossing totals can mean birds are dropping locally.

2

Identify Weather Stop Signs

Wind from the north or northeast, rain, storms, and cold-front passage can force migrants down. In many migration corridors, even light rain can turn a flyover night into a ground event.

3

Check Ground-Level Spikes

Use eBird to look for checklist surges, rare reports, or unusual numbers of migrants at local hotspots, parks, water sources, and river corridors.

4

Look For Field Cues

Tanagers, orioles, warblers, and vireos feeding at eye level, or several species crowding one tree or water source, are strong signs birds landed tired and hungry.

Fast Visual Cues

  • Tanagers, orioles, or warblers feeding lower than usual.
  • Multiple migrant species sharing one oak, mulberry, or water source.
  • Sudden activity after dawn following rain, north wind, or a front.
  • Quiet sky but busy trees: a classic sign birds stopped instead of passing over.
  • Birds working puddles, wet edges, or creek lines more heavily than usual.
  • Mixed flocks moving slowly through sheltered understory instead of high canopy.
  • Repeated arrivals at fruiting trees or feeders over a short morning window.

Peak timing varies by region and season. For spring migration in much of Texas, late April into mid-May is a useful watch window for fallout conditions.
